  <div class="max-w-3xl mx-auto px-5 sm:px-8 py-16 sm:py-20">

    <p class="font-display text-primary text-xs font-semibold tracking-[0.18em] uppercase mb-3">The guide</p>
    <h2 class="font-display text-3xl font-semibold text-navy tracking-tight mb-8">Understanding your mortgage, payment by payment</h2>

    <div class="prose-block space-y-10 text-[15px] leading-[1.75] text-ink">

      <div>
        <h3 class="font-display text-xl font-semibold text-navy mb-3">How this mortgage calculator works</h3>
        <p>A mortgage payment is rarely just one number. Lenders bundle several separate obligations into a single monthly bill, and this calculator is built to mirror that reality rather than simplify it away. When you enter a home price and a down payment, the tool first determines your loan amount — the portion of the purchase you're actually borrowing. From there it applies the standard amortization formula banks use to spread that loan, plus interest, evenly across your chosen term, whether that's ten, fifteen, twenty, or thirty years.</p>
        <p class="mt-3">But principal and interest are only part of the story. Most homeowners also pay property tax and homeowners insurance every month, usually through an escrow account the lender manages on their behalf, and many pay homeowners association dues or private mortgage insurance on top of that. This calculator adds all of those pieces together in real time, so the number you see reflects what will actually leave your bank account each month, not just the bare loan payment. Every field recalculates instantly as you type or drag a slider, and the results, charts, and amortization table update together so you can see the effect of a single change — a slightly higher rate, a slightly larger down payment — ripple through the entire picture.</p>
      </div>

      <div>
        <h3 class="font-display text-xl font-semibold text-navy mb-3">What actually makes up your monthly payment (PITI)</h3>
        <p>Lenders and real estate professionals use the acronym PITI to describe the four components of a typical monthly housing payment: Principal, Interest, Taxes, and Insurance. Understanding each piece helps explain why two loans with the same interest rate can have very different monthly costs.</p>
        <p class="mt-3"><strong>Principal</strong> is the portion of your payment that reduces the amount you owe. Early in a loan, principal makes up a small slice of each payment; as the balance shrinks, a larger share of every dollar you send in goes toward principal rather than interest. <strong>Interest</strong> is the cost of borrowing, calculated each month on your remaining balance, which is why paying down principal faster — through a larger down payment, a shorter term, or extra payments — reduces the total interest you'll ever pay. <strong>Taxes</strong> refers to property tax, assessed by your county or municipality based on your home's value, and typically collected monthly by your lender and held in escrow until the annual or semi-annual bill comes due. <strong>Insurance</strong> covers homeowners insurance, which protects the physical structure and is usually required by any lender as a condition of the loan, since the home itself is the collateral.</p>
        <p class="mt-3">Beyond PITI, many homes — especially condos, townhomes, and properties in planned communities — carry HOA dues that cover shared amenities and maintenance. Because HOA fees are billed directly by the association rather than the lender, they aren't technically part of PITI, but they still affect what you can comfortably afford each month, which is why this calculator includes them in your total.</p>
      </div>

      <div>
        <h3 class="font-display text-xl font-semibold text-navy mb-3">Private mortgage insurance (PMI) explained</h3>
        <p>PMI exists to protect the lender, not you, in the event you stop making payments on a loan with a relatively small down payment. On a conventional loan, lenders generally require PMI whenever your down payment is below 20% of the home's value, because a smaller down payment means more risk if home values dip and the lender has to foreclose. The cost is typically expressed as an annual percentage of the loan amount, charged in equal monthly installments, and it can range from roughly 0.3% to over 1.5% depending on your credit score, loan-to-value ratio, and loan type.</p>
        <p class="mt-3">The good news is that PMI isn't permanent. On most conventional loans, once your loan balance falls to 78% of the home's original value — through a combination of regular payments and rising home value — the lender is required to automatically cancel PMI. You can often request cancellation even earlier, once you cross the 80% threshold, by contacting your servicer. This calculator models that automatic cutoff, so you can see your PMI drop out of the monthly payment the moment your equity crosses that line.</p>
        <p class="mt-3">Government-backed loans handle this differently. FHA loans charge a mortgage insurance premium (MIP) that, depending on your down payment and loan term, may stay in place for the life of the loan rather than cancelling at 78% LTV. VA loans don't require monthly mortgage insurance at all, though they typically carry a one-time upfront funding fee. USDA loans charge an annual guarantee fee, billed monthly, similar in spirit to PMI. This calculator adjusts its PMI logic based on the loan type you select so the estimate reflects these real differences.</p>
      </div>

      <div>
        <h3 class="font-display text-xl font-semibold text-navy mb-3">Conventional, FHA, VA, and USDA loans compared</h3>
        <p><strong>Conventional loans</strong> are not insured or guaranteed by the federal government. They generally require stronger credit and offer the most flexibility on property type and loan amount, and they're the most common choice for buyers with a solid credit history and at least a modest down payment. Down payments can go as low as 3% for qualified first-time buyers, though anything below 20% brings PMI into the picture.</p>
        <p class="mt-3"><strong>FHA loans</strong>, insured by the Federal Housing Administration, are designed to make homeownership accessible to buyers with lower credit scores or smaller savings. Down payments can start at 3.5%, and credit requirements are more forgiving than most conventional programs, but that flexibility comes with mandatory mortgage insurance premiums that, in many cases, last for the entire loan term.</p>
        <p class="mt-3"><strong>VA loans</strong> are available to eligible veterans, active-duty service members, and some surviving spouses, guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs. They frequently allow qualified borrowers to purchase with no down payment at all and, notably, don't require monthly PMI, which can make the monthly payment meaningfully lower than a comparable conventional loan — though an upfront funding fee usually applies.</p>
        <p class="mt-3"><strong>USDA loans</strong>, backed by the U.S. Department of Agriculture, support homebuyers purchasing in eligible rural and some suburban areas, with income limits based on the local area. Like VA loans, USDA loans can allow zero down payment, but they carry an upfront guarantee fee plus a smaller annual fee billed monthly, functioning similarly to PMI.</p>
        <p class="mt-3">Selecting the right loan type in this calculator adjusts how PMI or MIP is modeled, giving you a more realistic side-by-side sense of how the same home price and rate can produce different monthly payments depending on the loan program.</p>
      </div>

      <div>
        <h3 class="font-display text-xl font-semibold text-navy mb-3">How extra payments save you money</h3>
        <p>Because interest is calculated on your remaining balance each month, any extra amount you put toward principal reduces the balance that future interest is calculated on — permanently. Even a modest extra payment, applied consistently, compounds over the life of a loan in a way that can shave years off your term and remove tens of thousands of dollars in interest, particularly early in the loan when the balance, and therefore the interest charged, is at its highest.</p>
        <p class="mt-3">Enter an extra monthly payment amount and watch three things change together: the total interest figure drops, the payoff date moves earlier, and the amortization schedule shortens. There's no prepayment penalty on most modern mortgages, so if your budget allows for it, even an extra $100 to $200 a month can meaningfully change the math — this calculator lets you see exactly how much before you commit to it.</p>
      </div>

      <div>
        <h3 class="font-display text-xl font-semibold text-navy mb-3">Reading your amortization schedule</h3>
        <p>An amortization schedule lists every payment over the life of your loan, showing how each one splits between principal and interest and what your remaining balance looks like afterward. Early in the schedule, interest dominates each payment because it's calculated on a large balance; as the years pass and the balance shrinks, the split gradually flips, until near the end of the loan almost the entire payment goes toward principal.</p>
        <p class="mt-3">This is also where the "crossover point" lives — the month when the principal portion of your payment finally overtakes the interest portion. On a standard 30-year loan at a moderate rate, that point often doesn't arrive until roughly the halfway mark, which is one reason financial planners often highlight the value of even small extra payments made early on: they pull that crossover point forward and reduce the total interest paid more than the same extra payment would later in the loan.</p>
      </div>

      <div>
        <h3 class="font-display text-xl font-semibold text-navy mb-3">Tips for getting a better mortgage rate</h3>
        <p>Because interest rate has an outsized effect on your total cost — far more than most buyers expect — it's worth the effort to shop for one. A higher credit score generally unlocks meaningfully lower rates, so paying down revolving debt and correcting any errors on your credit report before applying can pay off directly. A larger down payment reduces the lender's risk and often the rate itself, on top of helping you avoid or shorten PMI. Comparing loan estimates from at least three lenders, and paying attention to the annual percentage rate (APR) rather than the headline rate alone, helps surface fees hiding in the fine print. Finally, a shorter loan term almost always carries a lower rate than a 30-year loan, even though the higher monthly payment isn't the right fit for every budget.</p>
      </div>

    </div>
  </div>

/* ===================== Core amortization engine ===================== */
function runAmortization(p){
  const monthlyRate = p.annualRate/100/12;
  const n = p.termYears*12;
  const M = monthlyRate === 0 ? p.loanAmount/n : p.loanAmount*monthlyRate*Math.pow(1+monthlyRate,n)/(Math.pow(1+monthlyRate,n)-1);

  let balance = p.loanAmount;
  const schedule = [];
  let totalInterest = 0, totalPMI = 0, month = 0;
  const safetyCap = n + 480;

  while(balance > 0.5 && month < safetyCap){
    month++;
    const interest = balance * monthlyRate;
    let principalPortion = M - interest;
    if(principalPortion < 0) principalPortion = 0;
    let extra = p.extraMonthly;
    let totalPrincipal = principalPortion + extra;
    if(totalPrincipal >= balance){
      totalPrincipal = balance;
    }
    balance = Math.max(0, balance - totalPrincipal);
    totalInterest += interest;

    const ltv = p.homeValue > 0 ? balance / p.homeValue : 0;
    let pmiActive = false;
    if(p.loanType === 'va'){ pmiActive = false; }
    else if(p.loanType === 'usda'){ pmiActive = true; }
    else { pmiActive = ltv > 0.78 && p.downPct < 20; }
    const pmiAmt = pmiActive ? (p.loanAmount * p.pmiAnnualRate/100/12) : 0;
    totalPMI += pmiAmt;

    schedule.push({
      month, principal: totalPrincipal, interest, extra,
      payment: interest + totalPrincipal,
      balance, pmi: pmiAmt, tax: p.taxMonthly, insurance: p.insMonthly, hoa: p.hoaMonthly
    });
    if(balance <= 0.5) break;
  }
  return { schedule, totalInterest, totalPMI, payoffMonths: month, basePayment: M };
}

  const taxMonthly = (parseFloat(taxEl.value) || 0) / 12;
  const insMonthly = (parseFloat(insEl.value) || 0) / 12;

  const loanType = loanTypeEl.value;

  rateValEl.textContent = annualRate.toFixed(2) + '%';
  dpNoteEl.textContent = downPct.toFixed(1) + '% of price';

  else if(downPct >= 20 && loanType !== 'usda'){ pmiHintEl.textContent = 'Not required — 20%+ down'; $('pmiField').classList.add('opacity-50'); }
  else { pmiHintEl.textContent = loanType==='fha' ? 'Charged as FHA MIP' : loanType==='usda' ? 'Charged as USDA guarantee fee' : 'Cancels at 78% loan-to-value'; $('pmiField').classList.remove('opacity-50'); }

  const result = runAmortization({
    loanAmount, annualRate, termYears, extraMonthly, loanType,
    pmiAnnualRate, taxMonthly, insMonthly, hoaMonthly, homeValue: homePrice, downPct
  });
  lastResult = result;

  const first = result.schedule[0] || { principal:0, interest:0, pmi:0, tax:taxMonthly, insurance:insMonthly, hoa:hoaMonthly };
  const monthlyTotal = result.basePayment + taxMonthly + insMonthly + hoaMonthly + first.pmi;

  $('statLoanAmount').textContent = fmt0(loanAmount);
  $('statTotalInterest').textContent = fmt0(result.totalInterest);
  const totalTax = taxMonthly * result.payoffMonths;
